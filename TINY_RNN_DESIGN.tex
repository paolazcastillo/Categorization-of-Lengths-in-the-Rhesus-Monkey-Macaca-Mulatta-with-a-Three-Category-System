\begin{abstract}
This document specifies the two recurrent neural networks used to model the
behaviour of a rhesus monkey in a length-categorization task. Network 1 is a small
trial-level network (``tiny RNN'', in the sense of Ji-An et al.) that predicts, trial by
trial, the category chosen and its timing: it receives the previous choice, the previous
bar length, the previous outcome, the current bar length and the rule cue, keeps a hidden
state of one to four variables, and reads the choice out through two decision criteria on
the length axis. It is trained by maximum likelihood of the animal's choices and
compared, at matched number of state variables, with hand-written cognitive models
(model-free reinforcement learning, model-based reinforcement learning, a Bayesian
observer and a criterion-shift model). Network 2 is a gated network, at a 10 ms step,
that turns network 1's trial-level read-out into the cursor trajectory. Every decision
below is tied to what the task exports and to what the first sessions show.
Sections~\ref{sec:purpose}--\ref{sec:datareq} concern network 1; Section~\ref{sec:motor}
specifies network 2; Section~\ref{sec:within} ties the two together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Purpose and scope}
\label{sec:purpose}
%======================================================================

Four questions are asked of this design, and for each one the answer is a specific,
pre-registered procedure, not an impression from looking at fitted curves. A fifth
question, of the same shape, is stated separately in Section~\ref{sec:limits} because it
cannot be answered by this design at all.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{How much does the past weigh?} The categorization rule is fixed and the
      reward is a deterministic function of the stimulus, so no previous trial carries
      information about the correct answer to the current one. Any influence of the past
      is a bias. Does it exist, how large is it, and what shape does it have?

      \emph{How this is resolved.} By a chain of nested comparisons in held-out
      log-likelihood (Section~\ref{sec:models}): first M0 (no memory) against M1 (one
      trial of linear history) — if M1 does not beat M0, the past does not weigh at all;
      then M1 against the tiny RNN at $d = 1, 2, 3, 4$ — the smallest $d$ that matches or
      beats M1 is the answer to ``how large'', measured in state variables rather than
      trials. ``What shape'' is answered afterwards, by the diagnostics of
      Section~\ref{sec:interp} (criterion trajectories, phase portrait, gate statistics),
      never by the NLL comparison alone.
\item \textbf{What is the form of the update rule?} Compared with a criterion model
      written by hand (M2, Eq.~\eqref{eq:m2}), with the same number of state variables,
      does the network find a dynamics the hand-written model cannot express?

      \emph{How this is resolved.} M2 and the RNN are fitted on the same folds
      (Section~\ref{sec:split}) with $d = 2$ for both, and compared by held-out NLL,
      paired by session, with a bootstrap interval on the difference
      (Section~\ref{sec:models}, ``Decision rule''). A tie means M2's formula is already
      correct. A win for the RNN means the formula is wrong in a way that the closed-form
      fit of Section~\ref{sec:interp}, item 6, is meant to name.
\item \textbf{How does the 2- versus 3-category rule enter?} All at once, through the
      cue, or gradually, through feedback?

      \emph{How this is resolved.} Two versions of the same network are fitted, one
      with the cue as an input channel and one without it (Section~\ref{sec:rule}), and
      their fitted criteria are compared trial by trial around every block switch in the
      \texttt{alternate} sessions. A jump on the switch trial supports the cue-driven
      version; a multi-trial drift supports the latent one; the two are not mutually
      exclusive; the cue-driven version's own residual drift (if the cue-driven fit still
      shows a slower-than-instant adjustment) is itself the answer to whether the animal
      fully uses the cue.
\item \textbf{Does the decision state explain the movement?} The choice and its timing
      are one thing; the actual joystick trajectory that carries them out is another.
      Does knowing $\vect{h}_t$ change the prediction of the trajectory, beyond knowing
      only which target the animal walked to?

      \emph{How this is resolved.} This is network 2's question (Section~\ref{sec:motor}),
      with two nested controls (Section~\ref{sec:motor}, ``What is measured''): $\vect{b}$
      versus nothing, and $\vect{b}$ versus the single scalar distance-to-criterion $D_t$
      it is built to summarise. Section~\ref{sec:within} explains why the first control is
      expected to matter \emph{even under weak trial-to-trial memory}, because most of
      $\vect{b}$'s content is a same-trial, not a cross-trial, quantity.
\end{enumerate}

What network 1 does \emph{not} do on its own: it has no time resolution finer than a
trial, so it cannot say \emph{when within the 1.5 s of a trial} the category is settled,
only \emph{how long}, as a trial-level average (Section~\ref{sec:dt}). Modelling the
continuous within-trial formation of the decision would need a third, separate network,
at a 10 ms step; this design deliberately does not include one (Section~\ref{sec:two},
last paragraph, states what is given up by that choice).

\section{Why two networks, and what each one reads}
\label{sec:three}
\label{sec:two}
%======================================================================

The project has two recurrent networks because the behaviour has two time scales.
Across trials, the question is how the choice on one trial depends on the trials before
it, and how quickly and accurately it is carried out. Inside a trial, the question is how
that decision becomes a joystick movement. Each of the two has its own observable to
explain: the choice with its timing, and the cursor trajectory. Each therefore reads a
different file and a different set of columns (Table~\ref{tab:three}; the same two are
drawn unrolled in Figure~\ref{fig:unrolled}).

Network 1 is specified in Sections~\ref{sec:formulation}--\ref{sec:datareq},
network 2 in Section~\ref{sec:motor}, and the link between them in
Section~\ref{sec:within}.

Two consequences of this split are worth stating once. First, network 1 works on one
row per trial: everything it needs is in the trial table, and the one quantity that
comes from the trajectory (the takeoff heading) is reduced to a single number per trial
before it reaches the network. Second, network 2 never looks across trials: a trial is a
complete sequence for it, and the only way history reaches it is through $\vect{b}$,
which is entirely determined by network 1's read-out on that one trial. Network 2 is not
optional plumbing on top of network 1; $\vect{b}$ is the sole channel through which trial
identity and decision state reach the movement, and Section~\ref{sec:within} returns to
exactly what that channel carries and what it cannot.

\paragraph{What a third network would have added, and why it is not here.} An earlier
version of this design included a third, continuous-time network operating within the
trial (10 ms step) whose job was to read the category out \emph{as it forms}, giving a
mechanistic account of \emph{when} within the 1.5 s from bar to go the decision is
settled, and admitting a biologically constrained excitatory/inhibitory variant
comparable to extracellular recordings. Dropping it is a deliberate scope decision: those
two questions (continuous within-trial dynamics, single-unit circuit mechanism) are set
aside, and what survives of the timing question is answered at the trial level instead,
as a single number per trial (decision time, Section~\ref{sec:dt}) rather than a
continuous curve.

%======================================================================
\section{A question this design cannot resolve}
\label{sec:limits}
%======================================================================

Whether the animal categorises by comparing the bar to a \emph{boundary} (the view built
into every equation above) or by comparing it to a \emph{prototype} of each category and
picking the most similar one is a real, classical question in categorization research,
and this design cannot answer it from the data it uses. The reason is not a lack of
data; it is an identifiability result.

\paragraph{Why not.} On a single ordered continuum, a boundary rule and a
prototype/similarity rule with equal-variance categories make \emph{identical}
choice predictions: deciding by ``which side of $c_1$'' is mathematically the same
computation as deciding by ``which of the two nearest category means is closer'', because
the equidistant point between two equal-variance Gaussian prototypes \emph{is} the
boundary. No amount of choice data, however much of it, can tell the two apart when this
holds. The read-out of Section~\ref{sec:readout} is a boundary model \emph{by
construction} — it has two criteria $c_1, c_2$ and nothing else — so even the flexible
GRU behind it cannot discover a prototype-based strategy: the limitation is in the
read-out's functional form, not in $d$ or in the amount of training data.

\paragraph{What would make it resolvable, and why it is out of scope here.} A genuine
test requires either (a) an alternative, similarity-based read-out
($P(a_t = k) \propto \exp(-(\hat x_t - \mu_k)^2 / 2\sigma^2)$ for three learned prototypes
$\mu_k$) fitted alongside the ordinal one and compared by held-out NLL — informative only
if the two turn out \emph{not} to tie, which the equivalence result above says they will
whenever the underlying category variances are equal — or (b) a categorical-perception
discrimination sub-task (same/different judgements on pairs of bars, some crossing a
category boundary, some not, matched in physical distance), which is the standard way
the literature dissociates the two theories: a boundary account predicts sharpened
discrimination exactly at the boundary; a prototype account does not. CenterTask has no
such discrimination trial, so (b) is not answerable with the data this design uses, under
any amount of modelling.

\paragraph{What is partially informative, and already available.} The one asymmetry the
existing data can speak to is at the edge of the Short category, which has no lower
boundary (4.00 deg VA is the edge of the stimulus set, not a criterion). A boundary
account predicts 4.00 is the easiest, fastest Short trial, since it is furthest from the
only boundary that constrains Short. A prototype account does not have to predict this,
since 4.00 could be an atypical member of Short if the category's prototype sits nearer
its centre. Comparing decision time (Section~\ref{sec:dt}) across the four Short lengths
— flat, or graded with distance to the category's own centre — is a cheap, already-planned
analysis (it reuses the chronometric check of Section~\ref{sec:interp}, item 7) that
bears on this question without resolving it outright.

\subsection{Trial structure}

Each trial of CenterTask (\texttt{CenterOutTask.m}) shows a horizontal bar of one of
twelve lengths (4.0--7.8 degrees of visual angle), then a colour cue, then two or three
coloured targets at cardinal positions that are reshuffled on every trial. The subject
moves a joystick cursor from the centre to the target whose colour matches the bar's
category and is rewarded if the category is correct. Figure~\ref{fig:task} shows the
category structure; Figure~\ref{fig:sequence} the on-screen sequence.

\paragraph{Sequential effects are weak.} Accuracy is 0.788 after a correct trial and
0.692 after an error ($n = 2\,953$ and $913$). The probability of repeating the
previous category is 0.322 after a correct trial and 0.293 after an error, against a
chance level of $1/3$. The past therefore contributes little beyond a post-error effect.
This bounds what the network can find and motivates the small $d$ below.

\paragraph{The \texttt{alternate} regime is the base regime.} From 5 September onward
sessions run in session mode \texttt{alternate}: full 48-trial blocks of two and three
categories follow each other (the first such session ran 2, 2, 3, 3, 2, 2, 3). Accuracy
was 0.795 in the two-category blocks and 0.744 in the three-category blocks of that
session. This is the regime the network is designed for: identical stimuli, a rule that
changes within the day, signalled only by the cue, and a block switch every one to three
blocks. The design below assumes a corpus of \texttt{alternate} sessions of the same
size as the three-category corpus already recorded (12 to 15 sessions, 50 to 70 block
switches; the budget is derived in Section~\ref{sec:type}). The three-category-only sessions of August and early September are
kept as auxiliary training data (Section~\ref{sec:split}).

%======================================================================
\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}
%======================================================================

Each session is one sequence. At step $t$ the network receives $\vect{x}_t$, updates
its state $\vect{h}_t$ and outputs $\hat{\vect{y}}_t = P(a_t \mid \vect{x}_{1:t})$. This
is a many-to-many problem with $T_x = T_y$: one output per input step, with $T$ equal to
the number of trials in the session (240--465). It is neither many-to-one nor an
encoder--decoder (Figure~\ref{fig:m2m}).

The objective is the likelihood of the observed choices,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{lab}}}
  \sum_{\text{sessions}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} m_t \,
  \log P_\theta\!\left(a_t = a_t^{\mathrm{obs}} \,\middle|\, \vect{x}_{1:t}\right),
  \qquad N_{\mathrm{lab}} = \sum m_t ,
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $m_t = 1$ if the trial has a response and \texttt{Attempt} $= 1$, and $m_t = 0$
otherwise. The network is not trained to solve the task; it is trained to predict what
the subject did, errors included.

%======================================================================
\section{Type of network, size, and data budget}
\label{sec:type}
%======================================================================

This section answers, in plain terms, what kind of recurrent network is used and why,
what its size parameter $d$ means, which activation functions it contains, and how many
sessions are needed. The formal equations follow in Section~\ref{sec:arch}.

\subsection{Which kind of recurrent network}

A recurrent network is any model in which a state vector is updated at every step from
its previous value and the current input. Three standard cells differ in how the update
is written.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Vanilla (Elman) RNN.} One equation,
      $\vect{h}_t = \tanh(\vect{W}\vect{x}_t + \vect{U}\vect{h}_{t-1} + \vect{b})$.
      Keeping a value and replacing it go through the same weights, so a unit cannot
      do both well, and gradients through hundreds of steps vanish or explode.
\item \textbf{GRU (gated recurrent unit).} The same state, plus two gates that decide,
      per unit and per step, how much of the old value to keep and how much to
      overwrite (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gru-r}--\eqref{eq:gru-h}). One state vector, three
      weight sets.
\item \textbf{LSTM.} Two state vectors (cell and hidden) and three gates. More
      parameters, and the ``number of state variables'' is ambiguous because each unit
      carries two numbers.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The network is a GRU, not a vanilla RNN.} Three reasons. It is the cell used by
Ji-An et al., so the comparison with their results is direct. With $d = 1$ or $2$ the
gates are what let a single unit hold a criterion across trials and still reset it after
an error or a cue; a vanilla unit has to choose one behaviour. And it has one state
vector, so $d$ is unambiguous. The LSTM is not used because it doubles the state for no
gain at this size. A vanilla RNN with the same $d$ is fitted as a control
(Section~\ref{sec:models}): with short memory the gates may not be needed, and if the
two cells tie, the vanilla one is preferred because its dynamics are easier to read.

\subsection{What $d$ is}

$d$ is the number of hidden units, which for a GRU equals the dimension of the state
vector $\vect{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$, which in turn equals the number of dynamical
variables: the number of quantities the model carries from one trial to the next. In the
framing of Ji-An et al.\ this number, not the parameter count, is the measure of model
complexity, because it is what a cognitive model also has: the criterion-shift model M2
has $d = 2$ (two boundaries), the Bayesian observer $d = 4$ (mean and width of each
boundary), a model-free learner $d = 36$ (one value per length and category). Two models
with the same $d$ can be compared on equal terms even if one has twenty parameters and
the other eighty, because they remember the same amount and differ only in the form of
the update.

With $d = 1$ the entire memory of the animal, as the model sees it, is a single number
that moves up and down across trials. That is deliberately restrictive: it is the case in
which the whole dynamics can be drawn on a page. The prediction for this task is that
$d = 1$ or $2$ is enough, because the rule is fixed and sequential effects are weak.
$d = 3$ and $4$ are fitted to confirm that they do not improve held-out likelihood.

\subsection{What a hidden unit is here}

A hidden unit is one coordinate of $\vect{h}_t$: a scalar that is read from the previous
trial, updated with the current inputs, and passed on. It is not a neuron and it is not
a feature detector. In this network a unit is expected to behave as a slowly varying
bias, for instance the current displacement of a criterion from its resting value.
Units have no assigned meaning before training; their meaning is established afterwards
by reading how they move the criteria (Section~\ref{sec:interp}). That is the difference
between the $h_t$ of the network and the $V_t$ or $c_t$ of a cognitive model, which are
named before any data are seen.

\subsection{Activation functions, and why those}

There are three places where a nonlinearity sits, and only one of them is a choice.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Gates: logistic $\sigma$.} A gate is a proportion, how much to keep and
      how much to replace, so it must lie in $(0,1)$. The logistic function is the
      standard map onto that interval. Fixed by the GRU definition.
\item \textbf{Candidate state: $\tanh$.} The state must be bounded, otherwise it can
      grow without limit over the 300 steps of a session, and it must be symmetric
      around zero so that a criterion can move in either direction. $\tanh$ gives both;
      a ReLU would give neither. Fixed by the GRU definition.
\item \textbf{Read-out: logistic or cumulative Gaussian.} Each criterion turns a
      distance on the length axis into a probability, so the read-out is a sigmoid
      curve; it is the psychometric function of the model. Logistic and cumulative
      Gaussian are both used in psychophysics and differ only by a scale factor of about
      1.7 in the slope. The logistic is the default; the Gaussian is used when an exact
      match with the notebook's psychometric fits is wanted. This is the one choice.
\end{itemize}

No softmax is used: the ordinal read-out already yields three probabilities that sum to
one, and a softmax would discard the ordering of the categories.

\subsection{Size of the network and of the data}

The network has $d$ units, $k = 9$ (or 10) input channels and between 42 and 189
learned parameters (Table~\ref{tab:params}). Each session is one sequence of 240 to 465
steps, and gradients are propagated through the whole session. This is small: a fit
takes seconds, and the whole grid of models, folds and seeds fits in an afternoon.

The data requirement is set by three needs, and the largest of them decides.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Trials per parameter.} A rule of thumb for a well-conditioned
      maximum-likelihood fit is at least 20 labelled trials per parameter. With
      $d = 2$ and 85 parameters that is 1\,700 trials, about six sessions. The
      three-category sessions already recorded cover this for the choice model alone.
\item \textbf{Outer folds.} The paired comparison between models uses one difference
      per test session. Ten differences are the minimum for a bootstrap interval that
      means anything; twelve to fifteen are comfortable.
\item \textbf{Block switches.} The rule questions are answered on the trials after
      each switch. With segment lengths drawn from one to three blocks and about 320
      trials per session, a session holds
      $\approx 320 / \bar{L} - 1$ switches, where $\bar{L}$ is the mean segment length
      in trials. With segments mostly of one block ($\bar{L} \approx 65$) that is four
      to five switches per session; with segments averaging two blocks it is two to
      three. To estimate the post-switch curve on five trials, in both directions
      (2$\to$3 and 3$\to$2), with a resolution of about one trial in the time
      constant, at least 50 to 60 switches are needed.
\end{enumerate}

Need 3 dominates. \textbf{The target is 12 to 15 \texttt{alternate} sessions, with
segment lengths drawn from $\{1, 2, 3\}$ blocks with weights about $0.6/0.3/0.1$},
which yields four to five switches per session and 50 to 70 switches in total, and at the
same time satisfies needs 1 and 2. Ten sessions is the floor below which the latent-rule
version (Section~\ref{sec:rule}) cannot be fitted with confidence; the cue-driven
version and the choice-only questions are already answerable with the sessions recorded.
The three-category-only sessions add to need 1 but not to needs 2 and 3.

\subsection{State: a gated recurrent unit}
\label{sec:gru}

The state is updated by a GRU cell (Figure~\ref{fig:gru}):
\begin{align}
  \vect{r}_t &= \sigma\!\left(\vect{W}_r \vect{x}_t + \vect{U}_r \vect{h}_{t-1} + \vect{b}_r\right)
    && \text{reset gate} \label{eq:gru-r}\\
  \vect{z}_t &= \sigma\!\left(\vect{W}_z \vect{x}_t + \vect{U}_z \vect{h}_{t-1} + \vect{b}_z\right)
    && \text{update gate} \label{eq:gru-z}\\
  \tilde{\vect{h}}_t &= \tanh\!\left(\vect{W}_h \vect{x}_t + \vect{U}_h\,(\vect{r}_t \odot \vect{h}_{t-1}) + \vect{b}_h\right)
    && \text{candidate} \label{eq:gru-c}\\
  \vect{h}_t &= (1 - \vect{z}_t) \odot \vect{h}_{t-1} + \vect{z}_t \odot \tilde{\vect{h}}_t
    && \text{new state} \label{eq:gru-h}
\end{align}
with $\sigma$ the logistic function, $\odot$ the element-wise product, and
$\vect{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{panel_h.pdf}
  \caption{One GRU cell. The reset gate $r_t$ decides how much of the old state the
  candidate may read; the update gate $z_t$ decides how much of the state is
  overwritten; the new state is a per-unit blend of keep and replace. In this task,
  $z_t \to 0$ keeps a criterion where it was (long memory, the reward-learning regime
  of Ji-An et al.), $z_t \to 1$ rewrites it from the current trial (short memory, the
  regime the weak sequential effects predict), and $r_t \to 0$ lets the candidate ignore
  the past, which is how a rule switch can reset the state in a single trial.}
  \label{fig:gru}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The two gates, step by step.} A GRU unit does one thing per trial: it
decides how much of what it remembered to keep and how much to replace with a fresh
proposal. Read Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gru-r}--\eqref{eq:gru-h} in the order in which the cell
computes them.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The relevance (reset) gate $\vect{r}_t$ asks: is the past relevant to what
      I am about to propose?} It looks at the new input $\vect{x}_t$ and at the old
      state $\vect{h}_{t-1}$ and returns a number between 0 and 1 per unit. That number
      multiplies the old state \emph{before} the candidate is computed
      (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gru-c}). With $r_t = 1$ the candidate is built from the input and
      the full memory; with $r_t = 0$ the candidate is built from the input alone, as if
      the session had just started. It does not erase the memory; it only hides it from
      the proposal.
\item \textbf{The candidate $\tilde{\vect{h}}_t$ is the proposal.} It is what the unit
      would become if it listened only to this trial (and to whatever part of the past
      the relevance gate let through). It is a $\tanh$, so it lives in $(-1, 1)$.
\item \textbf{The update gate $\vect{z}_t$ asks: how much of the proposal do I accept?}
      It also looks at $\vect{x}_t$ and $\vect{h}_{t-1}$ and returns a number between 0
      and 1 per unit. Eq.~\eqref{eq:gru-h} then mixes: a fraction $z_t$ of the new value
      comes from the proposal, a fraction $1 - z_t$ is the old value carried over
      unchanged. With $z_t = 0$ the unit is frozen; with $z_t = 1$ it is overwritten.
\end{enumerate}

The two gates answer different questions and can take any combination of values.
$r_t = 0$ with $z_t = 1$ is a hard reset: the unit is overwritten by a proposal that
ignored the past. $r_t = 1$ with $z_t$ small is slow integration: the proposal uses the
past and only a little of it is accepted each trial. $z_t = 0$ is pure memory,
whatever $r_t$ does. A vanilla unit has only the last equation with $z_t$ fixed at one
and $r_t$ fixed at one, which is why it cannot both hold and reset.

\paragraph{In the words of this task.} Take $d = 1$ and read the single unit as the
displacement of the Short/Mid criterion from its resting value.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item On an ordinary correct trial the animal has no reason to move its criterion. The
      gates should give $z_t$ small: keep what there is.
\item After an error near the boundary, the criterion should shift towards the previous
      bar. The gates should give $r_t$ high (the proposal starts from the current
      displacement) and $z_t$ moderate (accept part of the shift). This is the
      criterion-shift rule of M2, Eq.~\eqref{eq:m2}, written as gate values.
\item On the first trial after a block switch, the old criterion is useless. The gates
      should give $r_t$ low and $z_t$ high: propose from the cue alone and accept it.
      This is the reset that lets a rule switch act within one trial
      (Section~\ref{sec:rule}).
\item Because the gates are functions of the input, the same unit can do all three on
      different trials. That is what the network has to learn, and it is what the gate
      diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:interp} read back out.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Read-out: ordinal with two criteria}
\label{sec:readout}

The categories are ordered along the length axis, so the output is parameterised as a
perceived length and two criteria rather than three free logits:
\begin{align}
  \hat{x}_t &= g(s_t, \vect{h}_t) = s_t + \vect{w}_g\T \vect{h}_t + b_g
    && \text{perceived length} \label{eq:ro-x}\\
  c_1(\vect{h}_t) &= c_1^{0} + \vect{w}_1\T \vect{h}_t
    && \text{first criterion} \label{eq:ro-c1}\\
  c_2(\vect{h}_t) &= c_1(\vect{h}_t) + \operatorname{softplus}\!\left(\delta^{0} + \vect{w}_2\T \vect{h}_t\right)
    && \text{second criterion, } c_2 \ge c_1 \label{eq:ro-c2}\\
  P(a_t \ge k) &= (1-\lambda)\,F\!\left(\beta\,(\hat{x}_t - c_k)\right) + \tfrac{\lambda}{2},
    \qquad k = 1, 2 \label{eq:ro-p}
\end{align}
and the category probabilities follow from the cumulative ones:
\begin{equation}
  P(a_t = \Short) = 1 - P(a_t \ge 1), \qquad
  P(a_t = \Mid)   = P(a_t \ge 1) - P(a_t \ge 2), \qquad
  P(a_t = \Long)  = P(a_t \ge 2).
  \label{eq:ro-cat}
\end{equation}

    Form & cumulative ordinal & Same structure as the criterion cognitive model and as the notebook's psychometric fit, which uses the ordinal splits $P(\text{chosen} \ge k)$ \\

\paragraph{Separation of biases.} Whatever enters through $g$ shifts \emph{perception}
and affects both rules equally (serial dependence on $s_{t-1}$). Whatever enters
through $c_k$ shifts the \emph{decision} (post-error shift, rule). This separation is
written into the architecture and is read directly from the weights $\vect{w}_g$
versus $\vect{w}_1, \vect{w}_2$.

\section{Two ways of knowing the rule}
\label{sec:rule}

The subject sees two or three dots in the cue and two or three targets on screen.
Giving that information to the network is not cheating: it models a subject who reads
the cue. Withholding it forces the network to infer the rule from feedback, which is
what the networks of Ji-An et al.\ do in reversal tasks. Both versions are fitted
(Table~\ref{tab:rule}, Figure~\ref{fig:rule}).

\section{Timing observables: decision time, execution time and movement takeoff}
\label{sec:dt}
%======================================================================

Every trial with a response yields, besides the category chosen, three measurements of
\emph{how} the response was produced (Table~\ref{tab:timing}). The model uses all three,
as inputs from the previous trial and as outputs on the current one.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering\small
  \caption{The three timing observables. The takeoff is the same event that ends the
  decision time (the transition from \texttt{Epoch} 6 to 7); what the trajectory adds is
  its direction.}
  \label{tab:timing}
  \begin{tabular}{L{2.3cm}L{5.0cm}L{3.4cm}L{2.9cm}}
    \toprule
    Observable & Definition & Source & Typical value \\
    \midrule
    Decision time $\mathrm{DT}_t$ & from target onset (go) to the cursor leaving the 200 px hold window & \texttt{DecisionTime\_s} & 351 ms (292--492) \\
    Execution time $\mathrm{ET}_t$ & from leaving the hold window to entering the 180 px target & \texttt{ExecutionTime\_s} & 183 ms (59--892) \\
    Movement takeoff & the moment the cursor leaves the hold window (its time is go $+\,\mathrm{DT}_t$) and its \textbf{heading} $\vartheta_t$: the direction of the cursor over the first 50 ms after leaving, measured as the signed angle to the chosen target's direction & derived by the dataset notebook from \texttt{X\_px}, \texttt{Y\_px} around the onset of \texttt{Epoch} 7; written to the per-trial table as \texttt{TakeoffHeading\_deg} & $0^\circ$ on a straight reach; large on a change of mind \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The three measure different things. The decision time is when the animal commits. The
execution time is the vigour of the movement once committed. The takeoff heading is
how committed the animal was at the moment of leaving: a reach that starts towards one
target and bends towards another is a change of mind, and it shows as a heading error
at takeoff before it shows in the endpoint. The takeoff heading is computed once by the
dataset notebook and stored as a column of the per-trial table, so network 1 still reads
one table with one row per trial.

\subsection{The previous trial's timing as inputs}

$\mathrm{DT}_{t-1}$, $\mathrm{ET}_{t-1}$ and $\vartheta_{t-1}$ are observed before trial
$t$, so they can be input channels without any leakage. They carry something $a_{t-1}$
and $r_{t-1}$ do not: how sure and how vigorous the subject was. A slow correct choice
and a fast correct choice are different histories, and the criterion shift may depend on
that. The channels are $\log \mathrm{DT}_{t-1}$, $\log \mathrm{ET}_{t-1}$ and
$|\vartheta_{t-1}|$, each standardised within the training set and set to zero on
trials without a response; the input dimension becomes $k = 12$. Each is tested with the
same ablation as the other channels.

What cannot be done is to use $\mathrm{DT}_t$, $\mathrm{ET}_t$ or $\vartheta_t$, the
current trial's values, as inputs for predicting $a_t$: they are part of the same
response that is being predicted.

\subsection{The current trial's timing as outputs}

The more informative use is to predict the choice and the three timing observables
jointly from the same state. Each gets a read-out from $\vect{h}_t$ and from the
proximity of the perceived length to the nearest criterion:
\begin{align}
  D_t &= -\min_{k \in \{1,2\}} \left|\, \hat{x}_t - c_k(\vect{h}_t) \,\right|
    && \text{proximity to the nearest criterion} \label{eq:dt-D}\\
  \mu^{\mathrm{DT}}_t &= \mu^{\mathrm{DT}}_0 + \kappa_{\mathrm{DT}}\, D_t + \vect{w}_{\mathrm{DT}}\T \vect{h}_t,
    \qquad \log \mathrm{DT}_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu^{\mathrm{DT}}_t, \sigma_{\mathrm{DT}}^2\right)
    && \text{decision time} \label{eq:dt-mu}\\
  \mu^{\mathrm{ET}}_t &= \mu^{\mathrm{ET}}_0 + \kappa_{\mathrm{ET}}\, D_t + \vect{w}_{\mathrm{ET}}\T \vect{h}_t,
    \qquad \log \mathrm{ET}_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu^{\mathrm{ET}}_t, \sigma_{\mathrm{ET}}^2\right)
    && \text{execution time} \label{eq:et-mu}\\
  \log \sigma^{\vartheta}_t &= \nu_0 + \kappa_{\vartheta}\, D_t + \vect{w}_{\vartheta}\T \vect{h}_t,
    \qquad \vartheta_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, (\sigma^{\vartheta}_t)^2\right)
    && \text{takeoff heading} \label{eq:th-sig}
\end{align}
with all $\mu_0$, $\nu_0$, $\kappa$, $\vect{w}$ and $\sigma$ learned. The decision time
and the execution time are log-normal with a mean that moves with the state. The takeoff
heading is zero-mean by construction (a straight reach) and what the state predicts is
its \emph{spread}: near a criterion, or after an error, the animal is expected to leave
the centre less accurately.

The loss of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} gains one term per observable,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{choice}}
  \;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{DT}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DT}}
  \;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{ET}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ET}}
  \;+\; \lambda_{\vartheta}\, \mathcal{L}_{\vartheta},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DT}} = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{lab}}} \sum m_t
  \left[ \frac{(\log \mathrm{DT}_t - \mu^{\mathrm{DT}}_t)^2}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{DT}}^2}
         + \log \sigma_{\mathrm{DT}} \right],
  \label{eq:loss-joint}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ET}}$ of the same form and
$\mathcal{L}_{\vartheta} = \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{lab}}}\sum m_t
[\vartheta_t^2 / 2(\sigma^{\vartheta}_t)^2 + \log \sigma^{\vartheta}_t]$. The weights
$\lambda$ are chosen on the validation session so that no term dominates (start at 1
with every term in nats per trial). The same mask $m_t$ applies: trials without a
response have no timing.

Three reasons to do this.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Four observations per trial instead of one.} With one categorical label
      per trial, the choice constrains the hidden state weakly. The three timing
      observables are continuous and constrain it far more. The joint fit is expected
      to identify $d$ and the criterion dynamics with fewer sessions.
\item \textbf{A direct test of the criterion interpretation.} If $c_1$ and $c_2$ are
      the subject's boundaries, decision times must peak and takeoff headings must
      scatter where $\hat{x}_t$ falls near them. That is the chronometric function of
      the notebook (Section 1.6), computed with the model's boundaries instead of the
      nominal ones. Equations~\eqref{eq:dt-mu} and \eqref{eq:th-sig} make the test
      explicit: $\kappa_{\mathrm{DT}}$ and $\kappa_{\vartheta}$ should be positive. The
      execution time separates decision from vigour: if $\kappa_{\mathrm{ET}}$ is near
      zero while $\kappa_{\mathrm{DT}}$ is not, difficulty slows the decision and not the
      movement, which is the expected pattern.
\item \textbf{A link to network 2.} Network 2 (Section~\ref{sec:motor}) produces a
      takeoff and an arrival as crossings of the hold radius and of the target when its
      predicted trajectory is simulated forward from $\vect{b}$ on a given trial; its
      simulated takeoff time, takeoff heading and execution time must agree with
      $\exp(\mu^{\mathrm{DT}}_t)$, $\vartheta_t$ and $\exp(\mu^{\mathrm{ET}}_t)$
      respectively. This is the only place the two networks are cross-checked against
      each other, since network 2 has no internal read-out of the category itself.
\end{enumerate}

To keep the comparison with the cognitive models fair, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{choice}}$
is always reported on its own, and the criterion model M2 receives the same three
timing read-outs, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:dt-D}--\eqref{eq:th-sig}, which it can because it also
has criteria. Models without criteria (M3) get the same read-outs with $D_t$ replaced by
the margin between the two largest $Q_t(s_t, \cdot)$.

\subsection{Elapsed time as an input}

The interval between trials is not constant: 2.0 s after a correct trial, 3.0 s after an
error, plus pauses. If the memory of the criterion decays with real time rather than with
trial count, a channel with $\Delta t$ since the previous trial lets the GRU learn a
time-dependent forgetting. The value comes from \texttt{Time\_ms} in the trajectory
export, not from the trial table. It is added last, and only if the ablation of
$r_{t-1}$ suggests decay.

\paragraph{Order of adoption.} First the three previous-trial channels as inputs,
because they are three more ablations and nothing else changes. Then the joint model
with the three timing outputs, because that is where the scientific gain is. Then
$\Delta t$, if needed.

\subsection{Gradient clipping}
\label{sec:clip}

Gradient clipping is kept even though the network is tiny, for a reason that comes from
the sequence length rather than from the size of the model.

\paragraph{Why the gradient can explode.} The loss is propagated backwards through the
whole session. The derivative of the loss at trial $T$ with respect to the state at
trial $t$ contains the product of recurrent Jacobians
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \vect{h}_T}{\partial \vect{h}_t}
  \;=\; \prod_{\tau = t+1}^{T} \frac{\partial \vect{h}_\tau}{\partial \vect{h}_{\tau-1}},
  \label{eq:jac}
\end{equation}
a product of up to 465 factors. If the recurrent weights $\vect{U}$ have a gain above
one in some direction, that product grows exponentially with $T - t$. The gates of the
GRU address mainly the opposite problem, the vanishing of that product; against
explosion they help less. The $\tanh$ bounds the state, not the gradient. With $d = 1$
or $2$ an explosion is less likely than in a large network, but one exploding batch is
enough to corrupt Adam's running moments for many epochs afterwards.

\paragraph{Why clipping and not truncation.} The classical alternative is truncated
backpropagation, cutting the gradient at windows of, say, 50 trials. That would remove
precisely the long-range dependence the model is meant to measure, the carry-over across
block switches. Clipping, by contrast, changes neither the loss nor its optimum: it
rescales the update only on the steps on which the global gradient norm exceeds the
threshold, and is inert on every other step. Adam's per-parameter normalisation does not
replace it, because that normalisation uses running averages which a single huge
gradient biases.

\paragraph{Diagnostic.} The global gradient norm is logged at every optimisation step.
The fraction of steps on which it exceeded the threshold is reported with each fit
(Table above). If that fraction is below 1\,\%, training is stable and clipping is only
insurance. If it is higher, the learning rate is too high or the threshold too low, and
the learning rate is lowered first; raising the threshold hides the problem. The teacher
network used for distillation ($d = 16$) is the fit on which the diagnostic is most
likely to fire.

\subsection{Data split: nested cross-validation by session}
\label{sec:split}

\paragraph{Why the unit is the session.} The state $\vect{h}_t$ runs through the whole
session, so consecutive trials are dependent by construction. A day also has effects of
its own that are not in the inputs: criterion drift, fatigue, joystick calibration. A
split by trial would let the model learn the state of the day from the training trials
and use it on the test trials of the same day, which inflates the likelihood and says
nothing about generalisation. A split by trial is defensible with tens of thousands of
trials per animal, as in the data of Ji-An et al.; it is not with about 300 trials per
day.

\paragraph{Corpus.} Two sets of sessions enter the fit. The \texttt{alternate}
sessions are the target corpus: every question of Section~\ref{sec:purpose} is answered on them. The
three-category-only sessions are auxiliary: they enter training with
$\cue_t = (0, 1)$ throughout, add about 3\,500 trials of psychometric structure, and are
never used as test for any question that involves the rule.

\paragraph{Outer loop: leave one \texttt{alternate} session out.} With $S$
\texttt{alternate} sessions there are $S$ folds. In each fold one \texttt{alternate}
session is the test set and is not touched during fitting. The remaining $S-1$
\texttt{alternate} sessions plus all auxiliary sessions form the fitting set. Test NLL
per trial, averaged over folds, is the number reported for every model. This measures
generalisation to a new day.

\paragraph{Inner loop: one validation session.} Inside the fitting set, one
\texttt{alternate} session is set aside as validation. It drives early stopping and the
choice of regularisation strength and seed. It is rotated over the $S-1$ candidates and
the resulting models are averaged in NLL, or, if compute is short, fixed to the session
closest in date to the test session. The number of state variables $d$ is \emph{not}
selected in the inner loop: $d$ is the quantity of interest, so every value is fitted
separately and its outer test NLL is reported.

\paragraph{Paired comparison.} All models of Section~\ref{sec:models} are evaluated on the same
folds. The difference in NLL between two models on each test session gives $S$ paired
values; the confidence interval is a bootstrap over those $S$ differences. Day-to-day
variation in absolute NLL cancels in the pairing.

\paragraph{Chronological check.} In addition to the random leave-one-out, a
forward-chaining pass: fit on all sessions before day $j$, test on day $j$, for every
$j$ from the third \texttt{alternate} session onward. If the chronological NLL is worse
than the leave-one-out NLL, the subject is still changing between days and the report
says so.

\paragraph{Secondary measure: blocks held out within a session.} For the dynamics
right after a block switch (Section~\ref{sec:interp}, items 1 and 2) the outer folds are complemented by
a within-session evaluation: in each \texttt{alternate} session, every second
two-category block and every second three-category block is masked out of the loss
while the network still reads it as input, and the NLL on the five trials after each
masked switch is reported separately. This is the masked trial-level split, and it is
legitimate here because the question is ``within this day, does the model predict the
trials that follow a switch?''. It is reported apart from the outer result, never
mixed with it.

\paragraph{The day-specific component.} The gap between the within-session NLL (blocks
held out) and the between-session NLL (day held out) is a direct measure of what is
specific to the day and not carried by the inputs. If it is small, the model transfers
across days. If it is large, there is a state of the day the network cannot infer from
the history, which motivates a per-session parameter or more sessions.

\paragraph{Cost.} With $S = 10$ outer folds, four values of $d$, ten seeds and eight
models the grid is about 3\,200 fits. Each fit takes seconds on networks of this size.

\subsection{Formal definitions}

\paragraph{M0, psychometric function without memory ($d=0$).} Equations
\eqref{eq:ro-x}--\eqref{eq:ro-cat} with $\vect{w}_g = \vect{w}_1 = \vect{w}_2 = 0$:
four parameters $c_1^0, \delta^0, \beta, \lambda$. Every gain is measured against this
floor.

\paragraph{M1, one trial back ($d=0$).} M0 plus linear terms in the previous trial,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}_t = s_t + \gamma_s\, s_{t-1} + \vect{\gamma}_a\T \mathrm{onehot}(a_{t-1})
              + \gamma_r\, r_{t-1} + \gamma_{ar}\, r_{t-1}\,\mathrm{sign}(a_{t-1} - \bar a),
\end{equation}
which separates ``there is an effect of the previous trial'' from ``the effect
accumulates''.

\paragraph{M2, criterion shift (the main cognitive model, $d=2$).} The two criteria are
the state. After each trial they move towards the previous stimulus if the choice was
wrong and decay towards a resting value:
\begin{equation}
  c_{k,t} = c_{k,t-1} + \eta\,(1 - r_{t-1})\,\operatorname{sign}(s_{t-1} - c_{k,t-1})\,
            \mathbb{1}\!\left[\,|s_{t-1} - c_{k,t-1}| < w\,\right]
            - \rho\,(c_{k,t-1} - c_k^{0}),
  \label{eq:m2}
\end{equation}
with learning rate $\eta$, window $w$ and decay $\rho$, and the read-out of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ro-p}. This is the analogue of the ``value'' update of Ji-An et al.,
written for perceptual criteria.

\paragraph{M3, model-free reinforcement learning ($d = 36$).} A table
$Q_t(s, a)$ over the 12 lengths and 3 categories, updated by the delta rule
$Q_t(s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}) = Q_{t-1}(s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}) + \alpha\,[r_{t-1} - Q_{t-1}(s_{t-1}, a_{t-1})]$
and read out by $P(a_t) = \operatorname{softmax}(\beta\, Q_t(s_t, \cdot))$. It has no
perceptual axis: a rule switch must be relearned length by length.

\paragraph{M4, Bayesian observer ($d = 4$).} A Gaussian posterior over each criterion,
$c_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$, updated by Bayes' rule with the likelihood
of the observed outcome, $p_t(c) \propto p(r_{t-1} \mid s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, c)\,p_{t-1}(c)$;
a noisy percept $x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(s_t, \sigma_s^2)$ combined with a prior $p(s)$
over lengths; and a decision that averages over the posterior,
$P(a_t = k) = \int P(k \mid x_t, c)\,p_t(c)\,dc$. The update is derived, not chosen;
serial dependence falls out of the prior.

\subsection{Decision rule}

The network ``discovers something'' only if it beats M1 and M2 with equal or smaller
$d$, in test NLL, with the bootstrap interval excluding zero. If it does not beat M1,
the past acts for one trial and does not accumulate. If it beats M1 but not M2, the
criterion model already captures the dynamics and is the model to report.

\section{Interpretation}
\label{sec:interp}
%======================================================================

Once trained, the network is read on the stimulus axis, not on the state axis.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Criterion trajectories.} For every trial, $c_1(\vect{h}_t)$ and
      $c_2(\vect{h}_t)$ in degrees of visual angle, plotted against trial number with the
      block switches marked. This is the empirical version of the lower plots of
      Figure~\ref{fig:rule}.
\item \textbf{Phase portrait in criterion units.} With $d = 1$: $c_k(\vect{h}_t)$
      against $c_k(\vect{h}_{t-1})$, one curve per input condition (correct/error
      $\times$ previous category). Where a curve crosses the diagonal there is a fixed
      point; its slope says how much is retained. This is the plot of Ji-An et al.\
      transferred to length units.
\item \textbf{Gates.} Distribution of $z_t$ and $r_t$ by condition. A mean $z_t$ near 1
      confirms short memory; $r_t$ near 0 on the trials after a block switch confirms a
      reset.
\item \textbf{Input ablations.} Remove $s_{t-1}$, $a_{t-1}$, $r_{t-1}$ or $\cue_t$ one at
      a time and measure the loss in NLL. This quantifies the three sources of the past:
      perceptual serial dependence, feedback-driven criterion shift, and rule.
\item \textbf{Perception versus decision.} Relative magnitude of $\vect{w}_g$ against
      $\vect{w}_1, \vect{w}_2$: whether the past acts on perception or on the criterion.
\item \textbf{Closed form.} If $d = 1$, fit a simple expression (piecewise linear or a
      low-order polynomial) to the learned update curve. If it differs from
      Eq.~\eqref{eq:m2}, it is a new cognitive rule.
\item \textbf{Chronometric check.} In the joint model, observed decision time,
      execution time and absolute takeoff heading against $|\hat{x}_t - c_k(\vect{h}_t)|$
      for the nearest criterion, binned. Decision time and heading spread must fall with
      distance (slopes $\kappa_{\mathrm{DT}}$, $\kappa_{\vartheta}$); execution time
      should not (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:dt-mu}--\eqref{eq:th-sig}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Network 2: the motor (joystick) network}
\label{sec:motor}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Question and data}

Network 2 asks how network 1's decision state becomes a cursor trajectory, and whether
that state explains the trial-to-trial variability of the movement (Section~\ref{sec:purpose}, question 4). One step is
10 ms; one sequence is one trial, from 300 ms before bar onset to 1\,500 ms after go,
about 335 steps (Table~\ref{tab:epochs}). Its data come from the full trajectory export
(\texttt{trajectory\_<runTag>.csv}, one row per cursor sample with \texttt{Time\_ms} and
\texttt{Epoch}) joined to the trial table on (\texttt{Block}, \texttt{TrialNumInBlock},
\texttt{Attempt}); the target is the measured cursor position, \texttt{X\_px},
\texttt{Y\_px}.

\subsection{Model}

\begin{align}
  \vect{b} &= \vect{W}_b\, \big[\vect{h}_t;\; s_t\big]
     && \text{trial-level read-out, $k$ dimensions, one value per trial} \label{eq:mot-b}\\
  \vect{g}(t) &= \mathrm{GRU}\!\left(\left[\vect{b},\ \mathrm{dir}_{\mathrm{onehot}},\ \mathrm{go}(t)\right],\ \vect{g}(t - \Delta t)\right)
     && \text{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gru-r}--\eqref{eq:gru-h}} \label{eq:mot-g}\\
  \vect{p}(t) &= \vect{W}_p\, \vect{g}(t) = (x, y)(t)
     && \text{cursor position, centre-relative} \label{eq:mot-p}
\end{align}
$\vect{h}_t$ is network 1's state \emph{after} it has processed trial $t$ (so it already
depends on $s_t$ through network 1's own forward pass); $\vect{b}$ therefore carries the
stimulus into the movement pathway only through the decision state, plus $s_t$ itself,
never through a separate direct sensory channel. Unlike a within-trial decision signal,
$\vect{b}$ does not vary with $t$ inside the trial: it is computed once, from network 1,
and broadcast as a constant input to every step of network 2's sequence
(Figure~\ref{fig:unrolled}).

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Decisions about the motor network.}
  \begin{tabular}{lp{5cm}p{7.5cm}}
    \toprule
    Element & Decision & Justification \\
    \midrule
    Source of $\vect{b}$ & network 1's $\vect{h}_t$ (plus $s_t$), never a continuous within-trial signal & There is no within-trial decision network in this design (Section~\ref{sec:two}); $\vect{b}$ is the only channel by which trial identity reaches the movement \\
    Bottleneck $k$ & the number of components for 90\,\% of the variance in the functional PCA of the movement curves, in the target frame; typically 3--5 & It is the actual dimensionality of the movement shape, and the number to compare with the neural population later \\
    GRU $M$ & 32 (sweep 16--64) & Two output dimensions, 3\,800 trials \\
    $\mathrm{dir}_{\mathrm{onehot}}$ & \texttt{DirectionChosen}, not \texttt{DirectionCorrect}, on from go & It is where the animal went, also on error trials. The layout is random and independent of the stimulus, so the direction carries no information about the category, and it is logged on every trial with a response \\
    Output & \textbf{position}, not velocity & Position is what is measured, every 9 ms. Velocity is derived and amplifies joystick noise \\
    Units & pixels relative to the centre, divided by the 406 px centre-to-target distance & The target sits at radius 1 and the hold window edge at 0.25, the same scale throughout the trial \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Why position and not velocity.} Four reasons, stated once.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The velocity in the export is obtained by differentiating the resampled position,
      and it needs a causal Butterworth filter at 10 Hz to be usable. That filter puts
      lag and design choices into the training target.
\item The sampling interval jitters (median 9 ms, 95th percentile 16.7 ms); a finite
      difference with an irregular step is noisier still. Position does not suffer this.
\item Movement onset and endpoint are defined in position by the task itself (leaving
      the 200 px hold window, entering the 180 px target), so the metrics are read
      directly from $\vect{p}(t)$ and coincide with \texttt{DecisionTime\_s} and
      \texttt{ExecutionTime\_s}.
\item Predicting velocity and integrating it accumulates drift: every step can look
      right and the endpoint be wrong.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Loss}

Squared error on $\vect{p}(t)$ over valid steps, with three terms.
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{motor}} = \frac{1}{N_v}\sum_{t\ \mathrm{valid}}
  \left\| \vect{p}(t) - \vect{p}^{\mathrm{obs}}(t) \right\|^2
  \;+\; \gamma \sum_{t} \left\| \vect{p}(t) - \vect{p}(t - \Delta t) \right\|^2 .
  \label{eq:mot-loss}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Before go} the target is the trial's actual hold position, not an exact
      zero: the cursor moves inside the 200 px window. The floor is the RMS of the
      position during HOLD, in pixels; a pre-go error below that RMS squared means
      nothing.
\item \textbf{After go}, ordinary squared error up to the arrival at the target.
\item \textbf{Smoothness}, the second term with a small $\gamma$, so that the network
      does not produce jumps. This recovers the one thing that a velocity target gave
      for free.
\end{itemize}
Curriculum: first the mean trajectories per length $\times$ direction, then single
trials. The first step fixes the shape; the second asks whether trial-to-trial
variability is explained from $\vect{b}$. Velocity, peak speed and curvature are
computed afterwards, from the predicted and the observed trajectory with the same filter,
and only as evaluation metrics.

\subsection{What is measured}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item $R^2$ of position per step and per trial; endpoint error in pixels; takeoff-time
      error, with the takeoff defined as the first crossing of the hold radius, as in the
      task (so it is compared with \texttt{DecisionTime\_s}); execution-time error, with
      arrival defined as entering the target (compared with \texttt{ExecutionTime\_s});
      takeoff-heading error over the first 50 ms after the crossing; and, derived from
      the trajectory, peak-speed and curvature error.
\item Against the linear decoder of the notebook at the same horizon: the GRU has to
      win, or the recurrence contributes nothing.
\item \textbf{Two nested controls}, both against the full model with $\vect{b}$:
      (i) the same GRU with direction and go only, no $\vect{b}$ at all — if this alone
      costs accuracy, \emph{some} decision-related signal reaches the movement, which
      Section~\ref{sec:within} argues is likely even with weak trial-to-trial memory;
      (ii) the same GRU with $\vect{b}$ replaced by the single scalar $D_t$
      (Eq.~\eqref{eq:dt-D}, already computed by network 1) instead of the full learned
      $k$-dimensional $\vect{b}$ — if (ii) matches the full model, $\vect{b}$ is not
      earning its keep beyond the obvious perceptual-distance signal; if the full
      $\vect{b}$ still wins, it carries something $D_t$ alone does not (plausibly the
      rule identity, or a genuine cross-trial bias). Together, (i) and (ii) are the
      direct answer to question 4 of Section~\ref{sec:purpose}.
\item Error trials (\texttt{ErrorType} $= 2$): is the trajectory towards the wrong
      target predicted from $\vect{b}$ before movement onset? This links to the
      changes-of-mind analysis of the notebook.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Coupling: how the two networks fit together}
\label{sec:within}
%======================================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:coupling} draws the coupling mechanism directly: network 1's read-out
on trial $t$, reduced to a small vector, becomes a constant input to every one of
network 2's $\approx 335$ within-trial steps, and network 2's simulated trajectory is
checked back against network 1's timing predictions. Figure~\ref{fig:unrolled} draws the
same two networks in the generic many-to-many layout used throughout this document. With
only two networks, the link between them is a single, mandatory channel rather than a
set of optional cross-checks, and the rest of this section states precisely what that
channel carries, why, and what follows for how $\vect{h}_t$ itself should be read.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{panel_j.pdf}
  \caption{The coupling pipeline. Network 1 processes trial $t$ and produces $h_t$;
  $\vect{b}$ is computed once from $h_t$ and $s_t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:mot-b}) and broadcast,
  unchanged, to every step of network 2's within-trial loop for that same trial. Network
  2's simulated trajectory, run forward from $\vect{b}$, is then checked back against
  network 1's timing predictions (dashed): this is a post-hoc consistency check, not a
  path gradients flow through in the two-stage training regime.}
  \label{fig:coupling}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Order and the channel}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Order.} Network 1 is fitted first, on its own, using only
      \texttt{trial\_data}. It is the cheapest, it fixes the minimal $d$ of history, and
      it settles questions 1--3 of Section~\ref{sec:purpose} before network 2 is touched.
      Network 2 is fitted second, in the two-stage regime and then jointly
      (Section~\ref{sec:motor}).
\item \textbf{The channel: $\vect{b}$.} Network 1's read-out $\vect{h}_t$ on trial $t$,
      linearly reduced to $k$ dimensions together with $s_t$, is the \emph{only} way
      trial identity and decision state reach network 2 (Eq.~\eqref{eq:mot-b}). There is
      no separate route by which the stimulus, the history, or the rule can influence the
      predicted trajectory. This makes the nested controls of Section~\ref{sec:motor}
      (network 2 with $\vect{b}$ removed, or with $\vect{b}$ replaced by $D_t$ alone) a
      clean test: whatever they cost is exactly what $\vect{b}$ was carrying, and the
      next subsection works out what that is likely to be.
\item \textbf{Consistency of the timing.} Network 1 predicts a decision time, an
      execution time and a takeoff-heading spread as trial-level statistics
      (Section~\ref{sec:dt}). Network 2, run forward with $\vect{b}$ from that same
      trial, produces a full simulated trajectory, from which the same three quantities
      can be measured directly (takeoff and arrival as crossings of the hold radius and
      the target). The two must agree, trial by trial; a systematic gap says one of the
      two networks carries a bias the other does not.
\item \textbf{What this design gives up.} Because $\vect{b}$ is constant across a
      trial's 335 steps, network 2 has no way to represent \emph{when within the trial}
      the decision was reached beyond what $\vect{b}$ already summarises; it can only
      act on a decision already made. A continuous within-trial account of decision
      formation is not available in this design (Section~\ref{sec:two}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What can network 1's hidden state plausibly detect, given weak sequential effects?}
\label{sec:hidden-content}

Section~\ref{sec:three}'s ``What the data show'' pools sequential effects that are
small: accuracy 0.788 after a correct trial versus 0.692 after an error, and
repeat-category probability within a few points of chance. It is tempting to conclude
that $\vect{h}_t$ therefore has little to do, and that the whole coupling built around it
is coupling to noise. That conclusion moves too fast, for a reason built into
$\vect{x}_t$ itself: \textbf{$\vect{h}_t$ is not only a memory of the past. It is
computed \emph{using} $s_t$, the current trial's own stimulus} (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}),
so it is shaped by something with no cross-trial component at all before any question
of memory arises.

Three different things can live inside a low-$d$ $\vect{h}_t$, at three different
timescales, and weak single-trial serial effects rule out at most one of them
(Table~\ref{tab:hidden-content}).

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Three candidate contents of $\vect{h}_t$, and how the design already tells
  them apart.}
  \label{tab:hidden-content}
  \begin{tabular}{L{3.6cm}L{2.6cm}L{8.4cm}}
    \toprule
    Candidate content & Timescale & How it is told apart \\
    \midrule
    Same-trial decision state: how far $s_t$ sits from the current criteria,
    $D_t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:dt-D}), i.e.\ how easy or hard \emph{this} trial's
    categorisation is &
    zero trials (within trial $t$) &
    Present even if $z_t \approx 1$ always (no real memory): $\vect{h}_t$ still differs
    trial to trial simply because $s_t$ does. Not weakened at all by weak sequential
    effects \\
    Rule / block identity: which of the 2-/3-category regimes is active &
    tens to $\sim$150 trials (a block) &
    A much \emph{longer}-range dependency than single-trial serial effects, and not

    (Section~\ref{sec:rule}) and by ablating $\cue_t$ (Section~\ref{sec:interp}, item 4) \\
    Genuine single-trial memory: a criterion shift that outlives one trial and decays
    (M2, Eq.~\eqref{eq:m2}) &
    one to a few trials &
    This is the one component the weak sequential effects (0.788 vs.\ 0.692) directly
    bound to be small; it is what survives \emph{after} removing the first two rows

  \end{tabular}

\paragraph{Reading the diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:interp} correctly.} Three
outcomes are all consistent with weak single-trial sequential effects, and they are not
the same finding.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item If $d = 1$ or $2$ does not beat M0 at all (Section~\ref{sec:models}'s decision
      rule), $\vect{h}_t$ has collapsed onto a redundant, same-trial function of $s_t$
      and $\cue_t$: the gates will show $z_t$ near 1 essentially unconditionally (nothing
      is ever protected across trials), and the network is, in effect, the psychometric
      function with extra steps. This is itself a reportable finding, already listed in
      Section~\ref{sec:predictions}.
\item If $d$ beats M0 but the gain tracks rule identity as a near step function between
      blocks, rather than evolving smoothly trial to trial within a fixed rule, the unit
      has learned \emph{which rule is active}, not a decaying memory of individual
      trials. The phase-portrait tool of Section~\ref{sec:interp}, item 2, is the wrong
      lens for this regime (it assumes single-trial dynamics); the criterion-trajectory
      plot of item 1, viewed across a block switch, is the right one.
\item Only a gain that survives \emph{after} ablating $\cue_t$ and that varies at the
      single-trial resolution \emph{within} a fixed-rule stretch is genuine short-memory
      dynamics, interpretable with the phase portrait and compared against M2 as
      originally intended.
\end{enumerate}
Conflating these three is the main risk in over-interpreting a small $d$: a unit that
is really tracking ``which rule'' or ``how hard is this stimulus'' can look, at a glance,
like it is tracking ``what happened last trial'', and only the ablations and the
block-aware plots above tell them apart.

\subsection{Consequence: what this predicts for network 2}

If the same-trial row of Table~\ref{tab:hidden-content} dominates, as the weak
sequential effects suggest it should, then $\vect{b}$'s value to network 2 is expected
to come mainly from \emph{within-trial decision difficulty} ($D_t$, how close $s_t$ sits
to a criterion) rather than from cross-trial memory. This is not a weakness of the
design; it is a well-grounded, independently motivated hypothesis in its own right: harder
perceptual decisions are known to produce slower, more curved, more hesitant reaches
(a documented effect of decision difficulty and confidence on reach kinematics), and that
effect requires no trial-to-trial memory at all, only a within-trial representation of
how ambiguous the current stimulus was. Two consequences follow directly.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The first nested control of Section~\ref{sec:motor} ($\vect{b}$ versus nothing) is
      \emph{not} expected to come back null merely because sequential effects are weak.
      $D_t$ is available every trial regardless of memory, and if the motor network uses
      it at all, that control will show an effect.
\item The second nested control ($\vect{b}$ versus the raw scalar $D_t$) is the one that
      actually tests for a contribution from the other two rows of
      Table~\ref{tab:hidden-content} (rule identity, single-trial history) beyond plain
      perceptual difficulty. Given how weak the sequential effects are, this second
      control is predicted to show little or no further gain in three-category-only
      sessions, and a larger one in \texttt{alternate} sessions right after a block
      switch, where rule identity is briefly the dominant thing $\vect{h}_t$ carries.
\end{itemize}

\section{Model skeleton in PyTorch}
%======================================================================

The choice loss is \texttt{-(mask * log probs[a\_obs]).sum() / mask.sum()}; the three
timing losses are Gaussian negative log-likelihoods of \texttt{log\_dt}, \texttt{log\_et}
and \texttt{heading} given the returned means and spreads, with the same mask, weighted
by $\lambda_{\mathrm{DT}}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{ET}}$ and $\lambda_{\vartheta}$. For the feedback-inferred version the two $\cue_t$ channels of
\texttt{x} are set to zero; for the choice-only model the second output is ignored.

\begin{lstlisting}
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class TinyRNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, k=9, d=1, link="logistic"):
        super().__init__()
        self.cell = nn.GRUCell(k, d)
        self.h0   = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d))
        self.w_g  = nn.Linear(d, 1)          # x_hat = s_t + w_g.h + b_g
        self.w_c  = nn.Linear(d, 2)          # c_1, delta  (c_2 = c_1 + softplus(delta))
        self.log_beta  = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))
        self.lapse_raw = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(-3.0))
        self.link = link
        # timing heads (Sec. Timing observables): each is bias + kappa * D + w.h
        self.w_dt, self.w_et, self.w_th = nn.Linear(d, 1), nn.Linear(d, 1), nn.Linear(d, 1)
        self.kappa = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(3))          # DT, ET, heading spread
        self.log_sd = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(2))         # sigma_DT, sigma_ET

    def cdf(self, u):
        if self.link == "logistic":
            return torch.sigmoid(u)
        return 0.5 * (1 + torch.erf(u / 2 ** 0.5))   # cumulative Gaussian

    def forward(self, x, s):                  # x: (T, B, k), s: (T, B)
        T, B, _ = x.shape
        h = self.h0.expand(B, -1)
        out, mu_dt, mu_et, log_sd_th = [], [], [], []
        for t in range(T):
            h = self.cell(x[t], h)
            xhat = s[t] + self.w_g(h).squeeze(-1)
            c = self.w_c(h)
            c1 = c[:, 0]
            c2 = c1 + F.softplus(c[:, 1])
            beta = self.log_beta.exp()
            lam = 0.1 * torch.sigmoid(self.lapse_raw)
            p1 = (1 - lam) * self.cdf(beta * (xhat - c1)) + lam / 2   # P(a >= 1)
            p2 = (1 - lam) * self.cdf(beta * (xhat - c2)) + lam / 2   # P(a >= 2)
            probs = torch.stack([1 - p1, p1 - p2, p2], dim=-1).clamp_min(1e-6)
            out.append(probs)
            D = -torch.minimum((xhat - c1).abs(), (xhat - c2).abs())   # Eq. (dt-D)
            mu_dt.append(self.w_dt(h).squeeze(-1) + self.kappa[0] * D)    # log DT mean
            mu_et.append(self.w_et(h).squeeze(-1) + self.kappa[1] * D)    # log ET mean
            log_sd_th.append(self.w_th(h).squeeze(-1) + self.kappa[2] * D) # log heading sd
        return torch.stack(out), torch.stack(mu_dt), torch.stack(mu_et), torch.stack(log_sd_th)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection*{Motor network: building $\vect{b}$ from the frozen network 1}

This is the two-stage regime (Section~\ref{sec:motor}): \texttt{TinyRNN} is trained and
frozen first; \texttt{trial\_b} below is run once per session to produce one
$\vect{b}$ vector per trial, which \texttt{MotorNet} then takes as a \emph{constant}
input, broadcast across every 10 ms step of that trial.

\begin{lstlisting}
@torch.no_grad()
def trial_b(tiny_rnn, x, s, W_b):
    # x, s: one session, shapes (T, 1, k) and (T, 1). Returns b: (T, k_bottleneck).
    T = x.shape[0]
    h = tiny_rnn.h0.expand(1, -1)
    hs = []
    for t in range(T):
        h = tiny_rnn.cell(x[t], h)
        hs.append(h.squeeze(0))
    h_seq = torch.stack(hs)                       # (T, d)
    return W_b(torch.cat([h_seq, s], dim=-1))      # (T, k_bottleneck), Eq. (mot-b)


class MotorNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, k_bottleneck=4, M=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.cell = nn.GRUCell(k_bottleneck + 4 + 1, M)   # b, dir one-hot, go
        self.g0   = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(M))
        self.w_p  = nn.Linear(M, 2)                       # (x, y), Eq. (mot-p)

    def forward(self, b, dir_onehot, go):    # b: (k,) constant this trial; dir/go: (T, .)
        T = go.shape[0]
        g = self.g0.unsqueeze(0)
        pos = []
        for t in range(T):
            x_t = torch.cat([b.unsqueeze(0), dir_onehot[t:t+1], go[t:t+1, None]], dim=-1)
            g = self.cell(x_t, g)
            pos.append(self.w_p(g).squeeze(0))
        return torch.stack(pos)                  # (T, 2)
\end{lstlisting}
